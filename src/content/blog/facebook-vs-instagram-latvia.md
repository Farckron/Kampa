## Who is actually on Facebook and Instagram in Latvia?

Facebook skews older and more local, Instagram skews younger and more discovery-driven. That split is directional, not precise: Meta does not publish reliable per-country age breakdowns, and the third-party numbers you find online are estimates built on ad-reach tools that overcount. So treat the following as a working assumption you verify against your own customers, not as data.

On Facebook you find most Latvians over 35, and more importantly you find the groups. Neighbourhood and city groups are the real asset here: "Jelgavas iedzīvotāji", district groups in Riga, parents' groups, buy-and-sell groups for a specific town. A single post inside an active local group routinely gets more real attention than a month of posting on your own business page, because groups still show member posts to members.

On Instagram you find most people under 40, and behaviour is different. Nobody joins an Instagram group to ask who fixes boilers in Purvciems. People scroll, save, and share. Discovery happens through Reels, Explore, and location tags, which means Instagram can put you in front of strangers in a way Facebook groups rarely do at scale.

## Which businesses win on Facebook?

Trades and services win on Facebook, and specifically inside local groups. Plumbers, electricians, window fitters, movers, car repair, cleaning, tutoring, childcare, appliance repair. The reason is that demand for these is triggered by a problem, and when a Latvian has that problem the first thing they often do is ask their local group who to call.

That changes what you do. You are not building a feed. You are becoming the person whose name gets typed into those threads. Join the five or six groups covering your service area, answer questions genuinely without pitching, and post about your own work rarely, following whatever the group rules allow. One useful answer under a "who can help with X" thread is worth more than any post you could design.

## Which businesses win on Instagram?

Cafes, beauty, and retail win on Instagram, because the product is visual and the purchase is impulsive. A coffee shop, a hair salon, a nail studio, a bakery, a clothing or ceramics shop: people decide with their eyes, often within a few seconds, and often for somewhere they will visit this week.

Instagram also handles the two things these businesses need most: showing what the place looks like before someone walks in, and letting a happy customer share you into their own network. A tagged story from a regular reaches people no ad targeting would have found.

## What should you post on each, and how often?

Set a cadence you can hold for six months, not one you can hold for three weeks. For a solo owner that means roughly this.

On Facebook: one page post a week, plus genuine participation in two or three local groups two or three times a week. Page posts should be practical, not promotional. Opening hours over the holidays, a job you just finished with a before and after, a straight answer to a question you get asked constantly, a price change explained honestly. Long text is fine here. Facebook readers tolerate paragraphs.

On Instagram: three posts a week and stories on the days you are open. Two of the three posts should be Reels, because that is where non-followers see you. Keep them under 15 seconds, shot on your phone, no music licensing worries if you use the in-app library. Stories carry the boring operational stuff: today's bake, a free slot at 15:00, the new colour that arrived.

If three a week is not sustainable, do two. A dead account that once posted daily reads worse than a quiet one that posts twice a week every week.

## Is a 35 EUR boost worth it?

A 35 EUR boost in Latvia typically buys somewhere between 6,000 and 12,000 impressions, reaching maybe 4,000 to 8,000 people, and produces roughly 40 to 100 clicks. Latvian CPMs are low by EU standards, usually in the 3 to 6 EUR range, but your actual number depends on targeting, creative, and season, so check your own Ads Manager after the first run rather than trusting this range.

Boosting beats organic page posting in one specific situation: you have something time-bound and geographically tight. A new location opening, a seasonal service with a deadline, a launch week. Then 35 EUR aimed at a 5 km radius around your address does something your page cannot.

Boosting is a waste when you use it to prop up ordinary content. If the post would not earn attention unpaid, paying to show it to more people just buys you a wider audience of people ignoring you. Spend the 35 EUR once a quarter on something that matters, not 12 EUR a month on nothing in particular.

## So which one should you pick?

Pick one primary channel that matches where your customer already is, and treat the other as a mirror at best. Splitting a solo owner's four weekly marketing hours across two platforms produces two neglected accounts.
